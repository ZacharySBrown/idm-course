Stop one. Where this sound came from.

[pause 500ms]

Palm founded PPG — Palm Products GmbH — in Hamburg around nineteen-seventy-four, building modular synths for acts like Tangerine Dream. His first piece of wavetable hardware was the Wavecomputer 360, end of nineteen-seventy-eight. Thirty wavetables. No analog filters at all. It sounded, in the contemporary account, buzzy and thin. Roughly forty were built. Palm published the term wavetable synthesis the next year.

[pause 500ms]

Here is what he was actually trying to do, and what went wrong, in his own words. Sound on Sound, MusicRadar — Palm: *"When I started developing the wavetable system, I wanted to simulate the typical low-pass effect, but then it turned out that these wavetable sweeps sounded very harsh; not at all like an analogue filter sweep."*

[pause 600ms]

He blamed the eight-bit hardware and the lack of real-time interpolation between waveforms. We'll come back to both — they turn out to be the entire sound. The fix that built the legend came later: *"I added the VCF to satisfy the customers who wanted that typical analogue sound. From then on, the success of the PPG Wave was unstoppable, because we had both — the new waveforms and the analogue filter sound."*

[pause 500ms]

So hold the one sentence that defines the technique. A wavetable is a row of single-cycle waveforms. You don't filter the sound to make it move. You walk between sounds. Palm's whole thesis, for what it's worth, was the opposite of nostalgia. His line, decades later — *"emulation is boring."*
