Third answer — the parts that exist to drive Position. The modulators themselves. Kept short, and kept pointed at the one destination.

[pause 500ms]

Three envelopes, one of which is the Amp envelope, the other two free. Each is graphical, with draggable handles for the start, the end, and the curve of every segment. The thing that matters here is the mode switch: None, Trigger, and Loop. None is a standard one-shot. Trigger plays through once and ignores note-off — percussive. Loop cycles continuously while the note is held. A free envelope in Loop, routed to Position, is a self-cycling timbral sequencer. Park that — Act six is built on it.

[pause 600ms]

Two LFOs: Sine, Triangle, Saw, Square, Sample-and-Hold, plus a Shape control that skews the chosen wave. Rate is free in Hertz or tempo-synced. And the underrated control — Attack, the LFO fade-in.

[pause 500ms]

A roughly two-second LFO Attack means the movement doesn't start when the note starts. It blooms in only after you've held the note — the comes-alive-on-long-notes pad trick. Name it, then hear it: slow LFO on Position, two-second Attack, one long note.

[cue: wt-lfo-attack-bloom]

Static at first, then the morph fading in — that was the LFO Attack. The pad that only wakes up if you hold it. Route an LFO to another LFO's Rate and you get motion that never repeats, but the fade-in is the one you'll reach for.
