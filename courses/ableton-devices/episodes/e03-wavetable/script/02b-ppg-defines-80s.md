The machine that made it pop was the PPG Wave 2, nineteen-eighty-one. The breakthrough was the hybrid Palm described — digital wavetable oscillators feeding analog voltage-controlled filters. Eight-bit oscillators, one analog filter chip per voice. Phrase the chips loosely, because the sources are loose: a Curtis-type filter on the Wave 2, an SSM-type on the 2.2 and 2.3.

The receipts. Tangerine Dream worked directly with Palm. Edgar Froese, to Sound on Sound, on the Wave 2 and the Waveterm companion sampler: *"The graphic monitor's representation of partial wave forms allowed us to create completely new musical structures."* Note that the famous *White Eagle* arpeggio is two Jupiter-8s, not the PPG — only the lead is the Wave.

[pause 400ms]

Gary Numan called the PPG Wave and Waveterm the heart and soul of *Berserker*, nineteen-eighty-four. Depeche Mode's was Martin Gore's, used in-studio on *A Broken Frame* — which is the choir and the bell riff you already heard. Here it is again, the glassy choir with the metallic bell on top. Notice the overtones moving inside the pad. Listen.

[cue: see-you-choir]

That is a wavetable scan — the timbre evolving while the chord holds. The canonical PPG sound.

Now the subtle one, because it's the better lesson. Tears for Fears, *Everybody Wants to Rule the World*, nineteen-eighty-five. A PPG Wave 2.3, a modified preset, layered under a DX7 bass. The DX7 gives the fundamental. The PPG gives a percussive high-end click — a transient, not a pad. Listen for the click on top of the low-end.

[cue: everybody-wants-bass]

That click is the PPG, used as attack design. Wavetable as a transient tool — keep that one in your pocket.

[pause 400ms]

Two myth-busters before we move, inline so they don't rot into lore. Bowie's PPG is on *Tonight*, nineteen-eighty-four — a credit, not a quote, and not *Let's Dance*. And the *Shout* choir hook is a Fairlight sample, not a PPG. Cite before you claim.
