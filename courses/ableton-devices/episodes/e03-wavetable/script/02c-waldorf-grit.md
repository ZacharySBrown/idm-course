PPG ceased operations at the end of nineteen-eighty-seven. The systems had run seven to ten thousand dollars and competed with the Fairlight. The grit, though, did not die with the company.

[pause 500ms]

Waldorf Electronics, founded in nineteen-eighty-eight by the former German PPG distributor, built the Microwave in nineteen-eighty-nine around an ASIC designed by Wolfgang Palm — though, to be precise, Palm was never employed by Waldorf. The line ran Microwave one, the Wave flagship, Microwave two, the Microwave XT with its forty-four orange knobs, then the Blofeld in two-thousand-seven. Sound on Sound traces the Blofeld straight back: descended from the Q and the Microwave two, which were themselves descendants of the PPG Wave.

[pause 600ms]

And here the aesthetic flips on its axis. Sound on Sound, Richard Leon, two-thousand-three, on the Microwave: *"This is lo-fi digital, 180 degrees away from the current fad for 24/96 quasi-perfection. The oscillators use eight-bit resolution instead of 12 or 16, and aliasing and other digital nasties are part and parcel of the Microwave's distinctive sound."*

[pause 500ms]

Read that again slowly. The aliasing — the thing Palm tried to filter out — is now named as the point. The strongest case from the decade: Charlie Clouser's Microwave on early Nine Inch Nails. His words — capable of *"the most brutal industrial bass sounds."* The grit had stopped being a defect. It had become a reason to buy the thing.
