The honest tradeoff. Operator has four operators. The DX7 had six. With six operators you can do parallel stacks — algorithm five on the DX7 is three independent two-op pairs summed at the output, perfect for layered electric pianos. The factory E.PIANO 1 patch uses one stack for the tine attack, another for the body resonance, another for the key thump.

[pause 500ms]

Four operators cannot do that. At best you get one or two parallel pairs, or one three-op stack plus a sine. The maximum spectral complexity that scales with cascaded modulators per carrier is genuinely smaller.

[pause 400ms]

For most IDM use cases — percussive plucks, sub-bass with bite, bells, FM hi-hats, glassy keys — four operators is more than enough, and arguably preferable. Smaller sound space, faster programming, lower CPU. The tradeoff you actually pay is in time-evolving spectral complexity. Workarounds: envelope loop modes inject quasi-random repeating modulations. Routing the LFO to operator levels gives slow spectral evolution. The Filter and Shaper sections add post-processing that classical FM doesn't have — Ableton put them there precisely to compensate for the four-op limit.

Robert Henke modeled Operator on his personal Yamaha DX27. That's why Operator is four-op rather than six. One man's daily driver became the synth a million Live users now take for granted.
