Yamaha's first FM hardware was not the DX7. The lineage runs through the GS1 in nineteen-eighty-one — sixteen thousand dollars, dual four-op, no programming — through the GS2, the CE20, the DX1 with its wood enclosure and thirteen-thousand-nine-hundred dollar list price. The GS1's most famous appearance is on Toto's *Rosanna* and *Africa*, nineteen-eighty-two, where Steve Porcaro layered it alongside a CS-80, a Prophet-5, a Minimoog, a Hammond, and a Polyfusion modular.

Then May, nineteen-eighty-three. The Yamaha DX7. One thousand nine hundred and ninety-five dollars. Six operators. Thirty-two algorithms. Sixteen-voice polyphony. Sixty-one velocity- and aftertouch-sensitive keys. Thirty-two RAM patches plus a ROM cartridge. The first synth with an LCD. The first synth to permit user-named patches.

[pause 400ms]

Within a year, orders exceeded one hundred and fifty thousand units. Two hundred thousand in three years. The first synth ever to break that barrier — Moog had sold roughly twelve thousand Minimoogs in eleven years.

That is ROM 1A — *E.PIANO 1*. Programmed in late nineteen-eighty-two by Dave Bristow and Gary Leuenberger in less than four days. It became the most ubiquitous keyboard timbre of the decade. Whitney Houston's *Greatest Love of All*. Chicago's *Hard to Say I'm Sorry*. Berlin's *Take My Breath Away*. Tina Turner's *What's Love Got to Do With It*. Phil Collins's *One More Night*. The *Twin Peaks* theme. By nineteen-eighty-six, the DX7 was on roughly forty percent of US Billboard Hot 100 number-ones and sixty percent of R&B number-ones.

[pause 400ms]

One chip. Three years. Fifty thousand transistors. The whole sound of a decade.
