The DX7 was not the only Yamaha FM machine that mattered. Below it sat the TX81Z, released in nineteen-eighty-seven, built around the YM2414B chip — also called the OPZ. Four operators, eight-voice polyphony, and the first FM synth to offer non-sine waveforms for its operators. It was a fraction of the DX7 price.

Its preset bank included patch C-fifteen, *Lately Bass* — named for Janet Jackson's nineteen-eighty-six track *What Have You Done For Me Lately*, not for any Reese-related reason. The patch was used by Babyface, then by Orbital on *Chime* in nineteen-eighty-nine, by Lil' Louis on *French Kiss* the same year, and on countless white-label house records through the early nineties.

A correction worth airing right here. The famous Reese bass — the one in every drum-and-bass track since nineteen-ninety-four — that is *not* FM. It was made on a Casio CZ-5000 using phase distortion synthesis, on Reese's nineteen-eighty-eight track *Just Want Another Chance*, then sampled into Renegade and Ray Keith's *Terrorist* in ninety-four. Phase distortion is FM's close cousin: instead of perturbing the instantaneous frequency, it warps the phase angle into a fixed waveshape. Same family. Different operation. Don't get it wrong on the internet.
