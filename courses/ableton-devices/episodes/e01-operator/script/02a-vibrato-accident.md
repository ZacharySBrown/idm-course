Chowning wasn't chasing timbre. He was chasing space. His doctoral work was on how the ear locates a sound in a room — direct sound, reflections, the physics of *being-here-not-there*. To study it he needed sounds with internal motion. Sounds that wouldn't sit still. Vibrato was the obvious tool.

He patched two oscillators on the university's mainframe. One vibrating the pitch of the other. He sped the vibration up.

[pause 400ms]

In his own words: *"I was experimenting with very rapid and deep vibrato. As I increased the speed and depth, I realized I was no longer hearing pitch."* When the vibration crossed the threshold between *a wobble you can count* and *a tone you can hear* — about twenty oscillations a second — the wobble vanished. In its place: a stable, brand-new timbre. Bright. Clangorous. Unfamiliar.

He later called it *"a discovery of the ear."*

The math turned out to be unreasonably economical. One sine wave modulating another, push the modulator into the audio band, and out comes a *fan* of new tones — partials spreading symmetrically above and below the original. We'll get there in a few minutes. The headline: this is not how synthesizers had ever worked before. There's no filter. There's no oscillator chorus. There's a pair of oscillators and a single number — modulation depth — and that one number controls the entire spectrum.
