Chowning wasn't chasing timbre. He was chasing space. He had studied with Nadia Boulanger in Paris, finished a doctorate at Stanford under Leland Smith in 1966, and was now alone in the SAIL dungeon working on the perceptual cues by which the ear locates a sound in a room. He needed sounds with internal motion so that direct and reverberant signals would phase-decorrelate. Vibrato was the obvious tool.

He patched two sine oscillators on a DEC PDP-10 running David Poole's local rewrite of MUSIC IV, called MUSIC 10. One oscillator modulating the other. He started doubling the modulator's rate.

[pause 400ms]

In Chowning's words to Yamaha's *Hub Magazine*: *"I was experimenting with very rapid and deep vibrato. As I increased the vibrato in speed and depth, I realized I was no longer hearing instant pitch and time."* When the modulating oscillator crossed roughly twenty hertz into the audio band, the cyclical pitch wobble vanished and was replaced by a stable but radically new timbre — bright, clangorous, evolving.

He later told MIT it was *"a discovery of the ear."*

[pause 500ms]

The mathematics turned out to be unreasonably economical. The standard radio-engineering equations for FM, written by Frederick Terman in his nineteen-forty-seven *Radio Engineering*, held perfectly well at audio rates. An undergraduate confirmed it. One sine wave modulating another's frequency, push the modulator into the audio band, and what comes out is a comb of new frequencies — sidebands above and below the carrier — with amplitudes set by Bessel functions of the modulation depth.

We'll get to the Bessel functions. They are friendly, and you have already met them.
