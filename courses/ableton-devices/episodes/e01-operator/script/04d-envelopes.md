Each operator has its own envelope. Plus the filter has one. Plus the LFO has one. Plus there's a global pitch envelope. Seven envelopes total per voice.

[pause 400ms]

Each envelope exposes ADSR — attack, decay, sustain, release — plus four levels: Initial, Peak, Sustain, End. Plus draggable slope curves per segment, linear or exponential. The exponential default mirrors DX7 behavior.

[pause 500ms]

The genuine power tool is the loop modes. There are five of them. *None* is standard ADSR. *Loop* re-triggers the attack-decay segment while the note is held. *Beat* re-triggers at a tempo-quantized division — sixteenths, eighths, dotted, triplet. *Sync* is Beat but quantized to the song-position grid. *Trigger* is one-shot, ignoring note-off — essential for percussive patches.

[pause 500ms]

What this means in practice: a modulator envelope set to Beat at one-sixteenth, with peak decay shorter than the loop period, becomes a built-in arpeggiator that lives inside the synthesis topology rather than outside it. No external sequencer required. You hold one note, and the modulator's brightness pulses on the grid, generating a glitch-pattern timbral wobble locked to the song tempo.

[pause 500ms]

There's also a long-standing forum-confirmed quirk in Operator's pitch envelope: routing it to the LFO destination accidentally modulates the LFO rate as well. Bug, or feature, depending on what week you ask. Forum thread two-one-seven-four-seven-nine on the Ableton forums confirms it.

[pause 500ms]

One more thing envelopes do — and it is the opposite of every pluck so far. Run the modulator envelope slow instead of fast. The harmonics fade in behind the fundamental, and the attack becomes a swell. Brian Eno's *An Ending (Ascent)*, from the *Apollo* soundtrack, nineteen-eighty-three, is the canonical case: a DX7 patch called *Glide* that Eno hand-built and published in Keyboard Magazine in nineteen-eighty-seven. There is no attack transient at all.
