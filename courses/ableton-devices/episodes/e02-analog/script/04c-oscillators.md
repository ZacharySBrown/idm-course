Next: what they kept. Start with the oscillators — the canon's source section, rebuilt clean.

[pause 500ms]

Two identical oscillators plus a noise generator. Each oscillator has four shapes — sine, sawtooth, rectangular, and white noise. No triangle; that's the deliberately classic, East-Coast palette from the history section. Octave, semitone, and a continuous Detune of plus-or-minus three hundred cents — and detuning oscillator two a few cents from oscillator one is the beating we proved earlier, the foundation of every fat patch.

[pause 500ms]

Three controls earn their keep. Pulse Width, active when the shape is rectangular, sweeps the duty cycle — and routed from an LFO it becomes PWM, the one-oscillator-as-two trick. The Sub control adds an oscillator an octave below; its shape follows the main one — square under a saw, sine under a sine — and it's the analog-bass weight knob, fundamental energy down low without a second oscillator. And there's an F1/F2 balance slider on each oscillator that routes its output between the two filters — a continuous balance, not a switch, which is how you build a split-filter voice later.

[pause 500ms]

Then there's Sync. In Sync mode the audible oscillator's waveform is hard-restarted by an internal, inaudible oscillator, and a Ratio slider sets how fast. Raise the Ratio and the harmonic content tears upward — the screaming sync lead. Ableton's own tip is to map a wheel to the Ratio for "maximum analog nastiness."

[pause 500ms]

Here's that Ratio swept up on one held note. Listen for a formant peak tearing upward while the fundamental pitch stays put. Listen.

[cue: an-sync-ratio-sweep]

That was hard sync, the Ratio sweeping. Note the fundamental never moved — only the harmonics, dragged upward by the restart rate. One thing they kept, and one of the loudest.
