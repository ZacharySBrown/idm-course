Now the filter, for a physicist — because you already know this from a lab bench, you just haven't heard it called synthesis.

Start with slope. A filter rolls off at six decibels per octave per pole. One pole, six. Two poles, twelve. Four poles, twenty-four. That's it — that's the whole relationship between the circuit's order and how fast it gets dark above the cutoff. Analog's "second order" filter is twelve decibels per octave; its "fourth order" is twenty-four. Cascade two twenty-four-decibel filters in series and you approach forty-eight.

[pause 500ms]

Then cutoff. The cutoff frequency is the minus-three-decibel point — the half-power point. Output power halved, amplitude down to one-over-root-two, about zero-point-seven-oh-seven. Yes — the same minus-three-decibel from your filter labs. The synth knob labeled "Freq" and the corner frequency in your transfer function are the same number.

[pause 500ms]

And resonance is Q. You feed the output back to the input, which boosts a peak right at the cutoff. Q is the center frequency over the bandwidth between the half-power points — a sharp, narrow peak is high Q. On a synth it's one knob called Reso, and it's doing exactly what feedback does to any resonant system.
