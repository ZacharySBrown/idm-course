The TB-303 Bass Line, nineteen-eighty-one, three hundred and ninety-five dollars, designed by Tadao Kikumoto. It was meant to be automatic bass accompaniment for guitarists practicing alone, paired with the TR-606 drum machine. A single oscillator — saw or square — into a resonant low-pass, with two per-step tricks that turn out to be the whole genre: *accent*, which deepens the filter envelope on chosen steps, and *slide*, which glues one note's pitch into the next.

[pause 500ms]

About that filter — this is genuinely contested, so I'll flag it instead of bluffing. It's usually described as an eighteen-decibel, three-pole filter, but Tim Stinchcombe's circuit analysis argues it's actually a four-pole diode ladder built from discrete components — diodes, not Moog's transistors, and the interaction of those poles is where the dirty, sharp squelch comes from. Eighteen or twenty-four, three pole or four — what's certain is it's a diode ladder, and it is *not* the IR3109. That chip was for the polysynths.

[pause 500ms]

It was a commercial failure. Discontinued in nineteen-eighty-four, roughly ten thousand made, dumped for around fifty dollars. And then Phuture — three kids in Chicago, formed nineteen-eighty-five. Spanky bought a discarded 303 for about forty dollars, and not knowing how to program it conventionally, they tweaked the knobs live. DJ Pierre's line is the whole lesson: *"Spanky was like, woah woah woah — keep doing that."*
