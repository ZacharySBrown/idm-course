Once Moog's ladder was the sound everyone wanted, the seventies turned into an argument about how to build a filter. Three companies, three answers.

[pause 500ms]

ARP first — Alan R. Pearlman, whose initials are the company. He founded ARP in nineteen-sixty-nine specifically because the early Moog and Buchla gear drifted out of tune, and he wanted something stable enough to sell. The ARP 2600, nineteen-seventy-one, was semi-modular — prewired inside, overridable with patch cords. Early units used the Model 4012 filter, which copied Moog's patented ladder closely enough that, after a Moog visit and a legal threat, ARP redesigned it into the 4072 in nineteen-seventy-seven. People call the 4012 the "lawsuit filter," but be careful repeating that — whether a suit was ever formally filed, or just threatened and settled out of court, is genuinely debated.

[pause 500ms]

Roland's answer was the IR3109 — a four-pole, twenty-four-decibel chip that went into the Junos and the Jupiter-8, the affordable end of the subtractive market. Hold that chip name; it matters in a minute, because the box this episode opened with does *not* use it.

[pause 500ms]

And Oberheim's answer is the one that matters most for our device. The Oberheim SEM, nineteen-seventy-four, used a voltage-controlled state-variable filter with low-pass, high-pass, and band-pass available at the same time, at twelve decibels per octave — and one knob morphs continuously between them. Notably it does *not* self-oscillate, which gives it a brighter, more open character than Moog's steeper ladder.

[pause 400ms]

That state-variable idea — one filter that can be low-pass or high-pass or band-pass or notch, your choice — is the closest hardware ancestor of Ableton Analog's per-filter type selector. When we open the device, that menu on the filter is Oberheim's argument, won.
