Who built the filter. Robert Moog, and a composer named Herbert Deutsch, in nineteen-sixty-three and -sixty-four. Moog wrote it up in an AES paper that October — *Voltage-Controlled Electronic Music Modules*. Alwin Nikolais watched the demo and placed the first order.

[pause 500ms]

The thing that gave the chain its voice is the filter. Moog's defining contribution is the transistor ladder, US Patent three-four-seven-five-six-two-three — filed October nineteen-sixty-six, granted October nineteen-sixty-nine. It exploits the exponential relationship between a transistor's base-emitter resistance and its current, so the cutoff sweeps exponentially over a roughly thousand-to-one range — which matches how we hear pitch. Four transistors in cascade give a twenty-four-decibel-per-octave low-pass. Feed the output back to the input and you get resonance, and pushed far enough, self-oscillation — a pure sine at the cutoff. Hold that last fact; it's the whole back half of the episode.

[pause 500ms]

Then the instrument. The Minimoog Model D — introduced nineteen-seventy, regular production nineteen-seventy-one, fourteen hundred and ninety-five dollars. Three oscillators, the twenty-four-decibel ladder, a noise source, one contour generator, pitch-bend and mod wheels — all on a fixed path, no patch cables. It was the first synthesizer sold in retail stores, ran thirteen years, and made over twelve thousand units.
