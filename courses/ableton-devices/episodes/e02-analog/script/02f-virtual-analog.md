Three reasons, and they're all complaints about hardware. Drift — analog oscillators wander out of tune as the circuit heats up. Cost — every voice of polyphony meant another physical oscillator and filter, so a polysynth was expensive. And recall — before microprocessor memory, you could not save a patch. You wrote your settings on paper and hoped.

By the mid-nineties, cheap DSP could model the oscillators and the filters in real time, and the complaints went away. Clavia's Nord Lead, nineteen-ninety-five, is the one that popularized the term "virtual analog" — though calling it the *first* is contested, because Korg's Prophecy shipped the same year. Then the Access Virus in nineteen-ninety-seven, the techno workhorse. And the Roland JP-8000, with its Supersaw — a single algorithm that stacks seven detuned sawtooths into one fat oscillator. Remember that one; Analog has a knob that approximates it directly.

The whole research problem is putting the drift and the nonlinearity *back* into a model that's too clean.
