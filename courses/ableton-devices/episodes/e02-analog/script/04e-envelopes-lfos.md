Now what they added — modulation no vintage subtractive synth actually had. Two beats of it, kept short.

[pause 500ms]

Envelopes first. Analog has four ADSRs — two filter, two amp, one of each per signal path. That's already generous; a Minimoog had effectively one contour shared between filter and loudness. But two switches are the modern part. *Free* mode bypasses the sustain phase, so every note has a fixed duration no matter how long you hold the key — that's the percussion switch, the drum-tone toggle. And *Loop* — AD-R, ADR-R, ADS-R — cycles the envelope. A looping filter envelope is a free, tempo-independent rhythmic wobble from a single held note. No vintage subtractive synth had that; it's closer to a modular's cycling generator, and it's the IDM glitch-pulse tool we use in section six.

[pause 500ms]

There's also S-Time — a sustain-stage decay that lets a held note fall like a plucked one without releasing the key — and a per-envelope linear-or-exponential slope, where exponential matches the natural RC-discharge curve.

[pause 500ms]

Then two LFOs, routable to oscillators, filters, and amplifiers. Sine, triangle, rectangle, and two kinds of noise — smooth random and stepped, the sample-and-hold "computer bleep" source. Rate switches between free Hertz and tempo-synced divisions. The pair of controls worth knowing are Delay and Attack: Delay holds the LFO off after note-on, Attack fades its depth in — together they make delayed vibrato, the human gesture of letting a note settle before the wobble arrives. And Retrig decides whether each note restarts the LFO phase or catches it free-running — free-run is the analog looseness, every note landing at a different phase.

[pause 400ms]

One more, hardwired: Vibrato is essentially a third LFO locked to the pitch of both oscillators, with its own rate and mod-wheel depth. Keep the looping filter envelope in your pocket. It's the rhythm trick at the end.
