And here's the question this whole section answers, planted now so the tour doesn't turn into a feature list. AAS had to fold the entire subtractive canon — Moog, ARP, Roland, Oberheim, the MS-20 — into a single device, and *then* make a perfect digital model sound imperfect. So: what did they keep, what did they add that no vintage synth had, and where do they hide the imperfection? Every beat in this section is one of those three answers.

[pause 500ms]

The origin first. AAS — Applied Acoustics Systems, Montreal, founded nineteen-ninety-eight by two PhD researchers out of the IRCAM tradition. Their modular environment Tassman pioneered analog-and-physical modeling, and the engine licensed into Ableton is their Ultra Analog. So unlike Operator — which Robert Henke built in-house in two-thousand-five as Ableton's own FM machine — Analog is borrowed physics.

[pause 500ms]

And the date matters, because it's a common error. Analog did *not* ship with Live 4 or 5. It debuted with Ableton Suite alongside Live 7, on the twenty-ninth of November, two-thousand-seven — the same Suite wave as Electric and Tension, both also AAS-derived.

[pause 400ms]

The upside of borrowed physics is the thing that makes Analog the right teaching instrument. Because AAS built it as a clean, generic model of the tradition rather than a clone of one machine, every control on the panel maps one-to-one onto the textbook chain we just walked through. The knobs *are* the physics. So let's go find them.
