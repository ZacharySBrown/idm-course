First answer: where they hide the imperfection. This is the philosophical knob, and it's the thesis of the whole instrument.

[pause 500ms]

Analog generates sound by physical modeling. The Ableton manual is blunt about it — no sampling, no wavetables. The mathematical equations describing how the analog circuits behave are solved every single sample, in real time, by the CPU. One consequence is that the oscillators and filters are alias-free: the sawtooth stays clean at the top of the keyboard where a naive digital oscillator would alias. Another consequence is the problem.

[pause 500ms]

The model is *too* good. It's dead stable. A real voltage-controlled oscillator drifts with heat and age and never quite holds pitch, and that instability is a large part of why analog sounds alive. A perfect model of an imperfect machine sounds wrong — clinical — until you add the imperfection back.

[pause 500ms]

That's the Error knob. It's a random, per-voice tuning deviation. Small Error is subtle warmth; large Error is drunk, detuned, vintage-broken. And it is the entire argument of the device in one control: a perfect digital model has to *add back* the drift that made analog worth modeling.

[pause 500ms]

Same sustained chord, twice. First Error at zero — dead still. Then Error dialed up — listen for the voices drifting against each other, a slow chorusing that isn't there in the first one. Listen.

[cue: an-error-drift]

That was Error, zero versus raised. The first chord was correct and lifeless; the second is wrong on purpose, and that's the one that sounds like a synth. Hold this — it's half the "make it analog" stack we land the section on.
