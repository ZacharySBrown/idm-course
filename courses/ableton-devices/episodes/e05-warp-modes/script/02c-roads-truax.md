Xenakis cut tape by hand. The next two names took that operation off the splicing block and put it inside a computer — and then made it run in real time.

[pause 400ms]

Curtis Roads was the first to realize granular synthesis on a computer, in nineteen-seventy-five. The early renders were brutal. By his own account it took weeks to render a single minute of mono audio. Weeks, for sixty seconds. If you want the canonical citation here, it is Roads's two-thousand-one book *Microsound* — that is the text the whole field cites, and it is where the history above is laid out properly.

[pause 500ms]

Then Barry Truax. Inspired by a Roads article, Truax built the first real-time granular system on a machine called the DMX-1000, and used it for a piece called *Riverrun* in nineteen-eighty-six. Real-time is the leap. Roads had to wait weeks; Truax could turn a knob and hear the cloud move while he turned it.

[pause 500ms]

So now you have the whole line. Gabor's theory in forty-six. Xenakis's tape in fifty-nine. Roads's digital render in seventy-five. Truax's real-time system in eighty-six. Four stations, forty years — and the line does not stop at Truax. It ends at a held drum loop in Live, running real-time granular synthesis on your laptop while you think about something else. The DAW is the last station on the line. You are standing on it.
