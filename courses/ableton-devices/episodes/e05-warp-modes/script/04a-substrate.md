Ableton put three unrelated DSP families behind one menu and the manual admits even the best mode is, quote, "never neutral — not even at the original tempo." So the question is not "what does each mode do." It is sharper: what runs *before* any mode does, which mode breaks *which* way, and which single control is the one that breaks it. Every beat from here answers a piece of that. This beat is the part that runs first.

[pause 500ms]

Before any mode touches the audio, Live analyzes the file. The manual: "when you first load a sample into a track, Live automatically analyzes the audio and finds its transients. These are amplitude peaks that indicate where notes or beats begin." Those become gray Transient Markers along the top of the Sample Editor; hover and a draggable Pseudo-Warp Marker appears, which promotes to a yellow Warp Marker — a committed anchor.

[pause 500ms]

Here is the sound-design lever hiding in that plumbing. Transient detection is the segmentation Beats mode loops between. The markers are where the blade falls. So if you deliberately mis-place a Warp Marker — drag it onto steady material, or quantize the lot to a grid that fights the real transients — you are telling the granular engine to cut in the wrong place. That is a direct corollary of how Beats works, and it is the first move in this whole episode: bad markers on purpose.

[pause 500ms]

Keep the manual's two lines pinned. One: the Complex modes use "an entirely different technology" from Beats, Tones, and Texture — the granular-versus-spectral split, said by Ableton itself. Two: even the best is "never neutral." Warping is always an effect. Transparent is the special case you usually fail to reach. The rest of the time you are doing spectral processing whether you meant to or not — so you may as well mean to.
