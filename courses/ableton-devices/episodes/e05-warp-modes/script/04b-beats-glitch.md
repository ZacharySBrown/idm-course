Beats is transient-locked granular. The manual says it is "optimized to preserve the transients in the audio," and the mechanism is the reason: it anchors its grain boundaries to those detected transients, and time-stretches by repeating or skipping the steady-state material *between* the hits. It never cuts through a transient, so the punch survives a stretch. That is the transparent use — match a drum loop to your tempo, keep the kick sharp.

[pause 500ms]

Now the three controls, because two of them are the breakage. Preserve sets the divisions Beats works between — "these divisions define the boundaries between portions of audio." Choose Transients for accuracy, or pin it to a fixed beat division: quarter, eighth, sixteenth, thirty-second. Transient Loop Mode decides what each segment does when the engine has to fill time. Loop Off: the segment "plays to its end and then stops." Loop Forward: it "plays to its end, then playback jumps to a zero-crossing near the middle of the segment and continues looping until the next transient." Loop Back-and-Forth reverses through it instead. And Transient Envelope is the fade between segments — a hundred means "no fade is applied," hard edges; zero means longer fades, smoothed.

[pause 500ms]

Stack the breakage. Loop Forward turns each slice into a held loop. Transient Envelope at a hundred keeps the edges hard and clicky. Drop the tempo to half. Every drum slice becomes a tiny frozen loop with sharp seams.

[cue: beats-stutter-freeze]

That is the classic Ableton stutter-freeze, and it is two settings away from transparent. Push it the other direction — set Preserve to a fixed sixteenth on a loop whose transients do not land on sixteenths — and the engine cuts mid-hit, manufacturing clicks and gating that were never in the source. This is Oval's skipping CD, automated. We come back to that in the last stop.
